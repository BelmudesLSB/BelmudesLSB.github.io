Working papers

  • Domestic vs. Foreign Law: Portfolio Dynamics of Sovereign Debt (Job Market Paper)

    With Angelo Mendes

    Emerging-market governments borrow both under their own law and under foreign law. If the government defaults, the governing law determines whether domestic or foreign courts settle disputes, and so how much investors recover. For twelve governments, we document that all borrow under both laws, that each issues its foreign-law debt at longer duration, and that issuing under foreign law costs a fee proportional to the money raised and independent of maturity. We show that the yield gap between a government’s bonds under the two laws widens with the probability of default. We build a quantitative model of sovereign default in which the government cannot commit to repay or to limit its future borrowing. Through the lens of the model, the widening gap implies that investors expect to recover more on foreign-law debt. The government still borrows under domestic law because shorter debt is less exposed to dilution. The fee keeps it from issuing shorter foreign-law debt, which would recover more and be less exposed to dilution. Calibrated to Colombia, the model implies recoveries of 47 percent under foreign law and 40 percent under domestic law. Removing the fee raises welfare by 12 percent of the gain the model generates when aggregate risk and default costs are eliminated, mostly through changes in how the government borrows and defaults.

  • Bank Runs and Market Value Solvency

    With Angelo Mendes

    Interest rate hikes lower long-duration government bond prices. Yet capital requirements do not take that into account and treat bonds as risk-free regardless of their maturity. This was a determining factor during the 2023 regional banking crisis. We study a version of capital requirements that assign positive risk weights to long-bond holdings and uninsured deposits in a dynamic model in which banks choose dividends and a portfolio of short and long bonds. Long bonds hedge fluctuations of deposit spreads while depositors stay in the bank, but make banks exposed to runs when rates rise. Uninsured depositors play a coordination game that makes runs likely when banks are poorly capitalized. Deposit insurance is financed by taxes on surviving banks, and affects depositors’ payoffs, changing their incentives to run. We use U.S. Call Reports for 2010–2025 to calibrate the model and match the cross-sectional demand for long-bond holdings. We then evaluate a capital requirement that imposes a floor on banks’ capitalization, based on their uninsured deposits. Along the observed rate path, a floor of 30 percent of uninsured deposits reduces deposits at failing banks by 15 percent. For at least half of the rate paths, however, the same rule increases deposits at failing banks, because it imposes losses after rates fall.

  • Transparency in Debt Crises

    With Angelo Mendes · Under revision

    We study a sovereign’s choice of whether to disclose information about its repayment capacity to international lenders in a 3-period Eaton and Gersovitz (1981) environment. Under full disclosure, debt is priced at actuarially fair rates for each type; under non-disclosure, a pooling equilibrium generates cross-subsidization across types. We show that non-disclosure is preferred when the probability of being the low type is small: the adverse selection discount the high type bears under pooling is then small, making opacity relatively cheap. Moreover, non-disclosure is preferred when deadweight losses from default are small: the insurance value of pooling then dominates the pricing gains from transparency. Two pieces of evidence support the framework. Mexico’s pre-1995 reserve-disclosure regime illustrates a durable, pre-committed disclosure rule consistent with the model. Using IMF Data Standards Initiatives, we document that no country has ever moved to a lower transparency tier, that countries facing higher recession risk are more often observed to adopt transparency tiers, an association consistent with the model’s threshold comparative static, and that early adopters exhibit, for two of the three tier margins, more dispersed residualized issuances than eligible non-adopters, in a direction consistent with the model.
